      “Even in the darkest of times we have the right to expect some illumination.”

      Hannah Arendt, Men in Dark Times (1968)

      I am at the TReNDS Center, a joint center of Georgia State University, Georgia Tech, and Emory University in Atlanta. I received my Ph.D. in Computer Science from Georgia State in 2025, advised by Vince D. Calhoun.

      I study what indirect observations can reveal about hidden structure: what an observation process preserves and what it erases, what can be identified at all and at what sample cost, what routine analysis choices cost, and when a result can be certified without ground truth. I work on these questions in three settings:

      1. 01 Latent-variable models

        Mixtures of independent sources (ICA) as the main model: how much signal a mixture leaves, what no estimator can recover, what normalization choices cost, and how to certify an estimate without the sources.

      2. 02 Partially observed dynamics

        Stochastic thermodynamics with hidden degrees of freedom: how much heat and dissipation a partial record can reveal, and which added measurement makes them identifiable.

      3. 03 Inference in brain imaging

        What template-based fMRI connectivity analyses can and cannot establish, from diagnostics that are blind by construction to robustness checks that cannot tell topography from coupling.

      1Separation Is a Riesz Energy: How Many Environments Does ICA Need?

      Yuda Bi*, Yubo Guo*, Jiaxiao Cai, Xiaonai Li, Zhuolin Li, Vince D. Calhoun†

      NewPreprint2026 · submitted to ICML 2027

      * Equal contribution. † Corresponding author.

      Central result

      Second-order statistics see source j only through its signature, the vector pj(1),…,pj(K) of its variances across the environments. Separating it costs the Riesz 2-energy of this point among the signatures: the sum, over the other sources, of inverse pairwise Fisher informations.

      The dimension law for well-spread signatures. Signatures live in K − 1 dimensions, so what counts is the dimension the configuration spans, not the number of environments.

      The question

      Independent component analysis recovers independent sources from a linear mixture, but i.i.d. samples of Gaussian sources determine the mixing only up to a rotation. Diversity rescues them: if the source variances change across K environments, be they segments of a nonstationary recording, time lags or datasets, the covariances share the mixing and the sources become identifiable from second-order statistics alone. Existing results are qualitative. They say when identification is possible, not how many samples it takes or which environments to collect, and the dependence on the number of components was open. The paper answers those questions with one geometric quantity, the Riesz energy of the signatures.

      Results

      • Theorem 1 (Separation costs a Riesz energy). For Gaussian sources and every n and d, no estimator in the orthogonal group recovers the j-th mixing direction with worst-case squared-angle risk below c Ej/(n + Ej). The energy is a sum of inverse pairwise Fisher informations, so the geometry of the signatures, not the number of environments, sets the difficulty.
      • Theorem 2 (Attainment by a quadratic pencil). A spectral estimator, the bottom eigenvector of Σk wk(Σk − pj(k)I)2, a matrix that vanishes only along source j, attains Ej/n up to a constant for every mixing, uniformly in d, once n ≳ K(d + log n)/Smin, and it may estimate the whitening from the data. A grid of such matrices also finds the signatures, given a lower bound s0 ≤ Smin (Theorem 3). In this regime the energy is the minimax rate (Corollary 1).
      • Theorem 4 (Dimension, not number). Counting how many signatures lie at each distance from pj gives Ej ≈ d2, d log d and d for K = 2, 3 and K ≥ 4 well-spread environments. Combined with Theorem 1, n ≳ d2, d log d and d samples are necessary for every configuration (Corollary 2), and K ≈ log d well-designed environments make d log d samples suffice. Eight environments whose signatures lie on a line behave like two.
      • Theorem 5 and Proposition 2 (Projecting versus probing). The eigenvectors of one linear combination Σk αkΣk, as in AMUSE, generalized eigenproblems and random pencils, have a first-order error whose mean over sources is ≳ d2/n for every K: the combination projects the signatures onto a line, where gaps shrink like 1/d. Probing one signature at a time avoids this, and the first-order error of joint diagonalization is again of Riesz type.

      Evidence

      At n = 400d, from d = 8 to 128, the mean row error of joint diagonalization grows from 0.14 to 0.52 for K = 2 and from 0.072 to 0.14 for K = 3, but stays between 0.018 and 0.022 for K = 8. For K = 4 the error stays within 0.86–1.09 times the first-order prediction up to d = 512. With K = 8 environments at n = 1600d and signatures confined to a subspace of dimension 1, 2, 3 or 7, the error follows the dimension: from d = 8 to 256 it grows from 0.055 to 0.68 in dimension 1 and stays near 0.049 and 0.035 in dimensions 3 and 7. Two configurations with the same smallest divergence but energies 3.8 times apart have errors 4.1 times apart.

      The sample size at which the error reaches 0.1 is 92–110 d for K = 8 and 188–205 d for K = 4, from d = 16 to 512, so n/d barely grows with d; a threshold that scaled as the proved bound would instead multiply n/d by 2.7 and 10.1. On fixed K = 4 lattices the mean error reaches 0.1 at 360–490 d from d = 27 to 1000. On identical data (K = 8, n = 400d) one linear pencil degrades from 0.47 to 0.84 while joint diagonalization and the quadratic pencil of Theorem 2 stay flat; as an initialization the pencil costs nothing.

      On 813 Human Connectome Project subjects with d = 200 cortical components and eight environments (seven tasks and rest), the energy ranks the split-half discrepancies of the components (Spearman 0.89, against 0.72 for the nearest-neighbour gap alone) but does not predict their size: with 400 subjects per half the mean discrepancy is 0.723 against a predicted 0.118, and a floor of 0.59 carries most of the ranking. Restricting to two environments makes the energy 53 times larger. The paper is explicit about the limits: the theorems assume Gaussian sources and equal environment sizes, the grid needs a known lower bound on the smallest divergence, the gap between necessary and sufficient sample sizes is open for fixed K, and practical algorithms are covered at first order only.

      2A 1/R Law for Kurtosis Contrast in Balanced Mixtures

      Yuda Bi, Wenjun Xiao, Linhao Bai, Vince D. Calhoun

      PublishedIEEE Signal Processing Letters, 2026  DOIarXiv

      Central result

      For any standardized projection, the magnitude of the excess kurtosis is at most the largest source-kurtosis magnitude divided by the effective width of the mixture, its participation ratio. Under balanced mixing the bound is order-tight.

      The question

      Excess kurtosis, the standardized fourth cumulant, is a central contrast of ICA and the basis of FastICA’s kurtosis-type nonlinearity, and it is known to fade as mixtures widen: the central limit theorem pushes wide projections toward Gaussianity. Earlier analyses took contrast strength as given. This letter measures how much contrast survives, at the population level, when a projection spreads its weight over many sources. That is the regime of high-model-order group ICA in neuroimaging, where more samples cannot restore contrast that the mixture itself has removed.

      Results

      • Theorem 1 (Sharp redundancy bound). For any standardized projection y = Σj wjsj, |κ(y)| ≤ κmax Σj w4j = κmax/Reff (Corollary 1). Under balanced mixing (maxj w2j ≤ cb/R) this is at most cbκmax/R, and equal weights with equal kurtoses attain |κ0|/R exactly. Proposition 1 shows that balance is generic inside well-conditioned blocks, with cb growing at most logarithmically in R.
      • Corollary 2 (Necessary model-order screening condition). The contrast of a balanced projection can exceed the estimation noise floor σ0/√T only if R < cbκmax√T / σ0, so doubling the tolerable width takes four times the data. The letter gives a coarse recipe that estimates this ceiling from data and flags hopeless model orders.
      • Theorem 2 (Purification lower bound). Keeping m ≪ R sources of one kurtosis sign and renormalizing their weights gives |κ(ypur)| ≥ κmin/m, with κmin the smallest kurtosis magnitude among the kept sources, whatever R is. A data-driven version picks the subset from a preliminary separation, without an oracle.

      Evidence

      Balanced mixtures of Student-t sources follow |κ̂(y)| ≈ 1.57/R (R2 = 0.994), and the estimator noise crosses the population contrast near T ≈ 3 × 104, as the screen predicts. Keeping m = 5 sign-consistent sources holds the contrast near 0.52 (oracle) and 0.59 (data-driven) from R = 20 to 200, while the full mixture falls from about 0.10 to 0.03.

      On resting-state fMRI from COBRE (n = 155), the per-subject kurtosis gap between the leading and the typical component time courses shrinks from model order 53 to 100 (paired one-sided Wilcoxon test, p = 1.74 × 10−27). The letter presents this as a supportive sanity check rather than a controlled test, since changing the model order also changes the signal subspace.

      3Zeros That Survive Mixing: Hardness and Immunity in Overcomplete ICA

      Wenjun Xiao*, Yuda Bi*†, Hanxin Chen, Sihan Jia, Vince D. Calhoun

      SubmittedIEEE Signal Processing Letters, 2026

      * Equal contribution. † Corresponding author.

      Central identity

      With independent sources, the characteristic function of the observations is the product of the source characteristic functions along the mixing columns. The product damps, and it cannot erase zeros.

      The question

      In overcomplete ICA, p sensors observe q > p independent sources. Pairwise non-collinear mixing directions are still identifiable for non-Gaussian sources, but two questions were open. How many samples does the problem need, for any estimator? And can the direction of one source of interest be estimated without modeling the others? The letter answers both from the product identity above.

      Results

      • Theorem 1 (Aliasing barrier). With two sensors and q equally spaced sources sharing a smooth symmetric law (Gram–Charlier laws, or binary symbols in Gaussian noise), a rotation by π/(2q) leaves every cumulant of order below 2q unchanged and moves the observation law exponentially little in total variation. Resolving the directions therefore takes eΘ(q) samples, necessary for every estimator and sufficient for a family of sources; for the Gram–Charlier law φ4, 1.5288q − O(log q) ≤ log n*(q) ≤ 2.3689q + O(1). Already at q = 10 the squared Hellinger distance is 4.1 × 10−9, so at least 6 × 107 samples are needed.
      • Theorem 2 (Immunity). If the characteristic function of a target source has a real zero, as for equiprobable binary or pulse-amplitude symbols, complex-exponential moment conditions on its mixing direction are exactly unbiased, whatever the number, strength, laws, and mutual dependence of the other sources, provided they are independent of the target. Each such source leaves zero lines in the observed characteristic function that no other source can remove (Corollary 1); the zero-line estimator CFZ locates the target’s line within a prior window that contains no other source with such zeros.
      • Propositions 1 and 2 (Moments are immune, variance is not; Price of local immunity). Immunity covers the moments, not the variance: for complex-exponential (Fourier) moments, each added interferer multiplies the Jacobian of the moment conditions by a factor of modulus at most one. Locally, an estimator that must stay unbiased against arbitrary weak interference keeps a computable share of the information: zero for Gaussian and Laplace sources, above 90% for binary symbols in Gaussian noise at 10 dB.

      Evidence

      The simulations estimate the direction of a binary target at 17°, seen by a two-axis vector sensor at 10 dB among Gaussian, four-level PAM, impulsive (Bernoulli–Gaussian), and heavy-tailed Student-t interferers. At n = 105 snapshots the RMSE of CFZ is 0.15°–0.40° across seven settings, its standard deviation within 10% of the prediction of Proposition 1, with no error above 3°.

      Baselines with a fixed interference model level off instead: semi-blind likelihood with a Gaussian model (SB-G) and a kurtosis contrast under mixed or heavy-tailed interference, and a Student-t model (SB-T) under mixed interference at the lower power, with errors of up to 8.4°. A Gaussian-mixture likelihood matches or beats CFZ at one to two orders of magnitude more computation: where a model is adequate, likelihood is the more efficient route; immunity is what remains when none is.

      4Normalization Mismatch in ICA and Its Orthogonalized Remedy

      Jingling Yang*, Yuda Bi*†, Linhao Bai, Vince D. Calhoun

      SubmittedIEEE Transactions on Signal Processing, 2026

      * Equal contribution. † Corresponding author.

      Central result

      The exact first-order cost of a protocol that scores records with weights A, scales with W, and centers with U. The last two terms are paid only when the normalizing records differ from the scored ones.

      The orthogonalized contrast. With κ and ϑ estimated consistently from the normalizing records alone, it closes the scale and location channels and leaves matched-role fits unchanged.

      The question

      Some pipelines normalize an ICA criterion with statistics from records other than the ones that score it. FastICA’s sampleSize option scores a random fraction of the records while centering and whitening on all of them; real-time EEG pipelines keep the sphere of a calibration run; cross-fitting and reliability analyses refit on split or resampled records. The paper asks what this normalization mismatch costs, exactly, for one-unit and for symmetric fits; whether the cost can be removed without abandoning the protocol; and what agreement among differently normalized fits says about accuracy.

      Results

      • Theorem 1 (Local three-role influence law). Normalization is not orthogonal at first order, unlike the source density and smooth score parameters (Remark 1). For a one-unit fit with arbitrary scoring, scale, and centering weights, the influence function splits into score exposure, a scale channel, and a location channel, which gives the exact first-order cost above. Equal-size cross-fitting costs nothing; 10–20% reference or calibration normalizers cost up to hundreds of times the common-sample variance for log-cosh on sub-Gaussian targets.
      • Theorem 3 (Symmetric fits under mismatch). For symmetric FastICA the law is pairwise. A pair pays through the difference of its sign-corrected scale weights, large for opposite curvature signs under log-cosh, and through the whitening’s own effective sample size, even when the roles match.
      • Theorems 2 and 4 (Orthogonalized contrast; Orthogonalized symmetric contrast). Subtracting consistently estimated linear and quadratic terms removes the mismatch channels for one-unit and symmetric fits and leaves matched-role fits unchanged. What remains is the price of scoring fewer records and, for symmetric fits, the whitening’s effective sample size. Balanced reuse of the records reaches the common-sample law without any source moments (Corollary 2).
      • Corollaries 3, 4, and 6 (Two-point targets; Exponential windows; Asymptotic localized confidence cone). For two-point targets such as BPSK, mismatch changes the rate from exact recovery to N−1/2. With exponentially weighted roles, the whitening memory should be the longer one. No remedy reduces the common sampling error, so agreement among differently normalized fits on the same scored records is blind to it; the paper gives an observable confidence cone to report instead.

      Evidence

      Five real signals (an electrocardiogram from the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database, two natural images, and the horizontal gradients of two more) are mixed by known random matrices. Under 10% reference or 20% calibration whitening, plain one-unit refits pay 9.9 to 728 times the common-sample variance and plain symmetric fits 22 to 71 times their common-sample loss, in line with the first-order predictions. Orthogonalized one-unit refits pay 1.01–1.09 and 1.23–1.37 times it, against the predicted 1 and 1.25. Across the reference, calibration, and subsample protocols, the symmetric remedy cuts every loss by 6 to 14 times.

      In a line-by-line port of the FastICA sampleSize code path with a 10% subsample, the default deflation mode returned no component in any of 100 runs, and the redrawn-subset symmetric iteration with tanh met its stopping test in only 5 of 100. Patching four lines with the orthogonalized nonlinearity brought the tanh losses to the predicted floor and its stopping rate to 100 of 100. With pow3, the package’s default nonlinearity, the patch also brings the fixed-subset losses close to the floor, but the redrawn iteration stops in only 13 of 100 runs.

      5Sharp Two-Sided Residual Certificates for Comparing ICA Estimates Without the Sources

      Yuda Bi, Hanxin Chen, Sihan Jia, Zhaoxing Wei†

      Under review2026

      † Corresponding author.

      Central result

      For an orthogonal fourth-cumulant model, the off-diagonal residual r of a candidate brackets its distance D to the sources: from below with an optimal constant, and, for a candidate known to lie within distance D̄ < √2 of them, from above with a coefficient K(η) that tends to the optimal local value 1/√2. With equal-magnitude cumulants the two sides meet near the sources.

      The question

      An ICA run returns a demixing matrix but no measure of its accuracy. Since the sources are unknown, the natural source-free diagnostic is how well the estimate diagonalizes an observed statistic; for the fourth-order cumulant tensor, that is the off-diagonal residual. Upper bounds turn the residual into an error bar for one estimate, but deciding which of two estimates is closer to the sources also needs a computable lower bound. The question is practical in template-guided pipelines, where a guided estimate matches its template better than a data-only fit by construction, which says nothing about its distance to the sources.

      Results

      • Theorem 1 (Optimal global reverse bound). For every orthogonal candidate B and cumulants of any sign, r(B) ≤ √2 Γ D(B), where D is the distance to the signed-permutation orbit of the sources and Γ the root-sum-square of the two largest cumulant magnitudes. No smaller constant holds for all candidates (whenever the cumulant tensor is nonzero), and the bound improves on the column-wise constant by a factor of up to √d.
      • Theorem 2 (Finite, locally sharp upper bound). If γ > 0 and the candidate is known to satisfy D(B) ≤ D̄ < √2, then D(B) ≤ K(η) r(B)/γ with η = 1 − D̄2/2; the coefficient decreases to the optimal local value 1/√2 as D̄ → 0.
      • Theorem 3 (Observable bracket). A spectral estimate of Γ from the d2 × d2 unfolding of the observed tensor makes both sides computable under a valid tensor-error radius δ, giving L(B) ≤ D(B) ≤ C(B). With an exact tensor (δ = 0) the bracket is asymptotically exact near the source orbit for equal-magnitude cumulants (Corollary 1), and C(B) < L(V) certifies that B is closer to the sources than V.

      Evidence

      In a paired ablation with the same tensors, radii, and candidates, the new bounds raise the certified share of candidates truly closer than their reference from 22.6% to 45.0%, with no false verdicts. Median looseness C/D falls from 8.55 to 6.06, and across 3,360 exact-tensor comparisons the certified count rises from 132 to 1,253, again with no false claims.

      The paper is explicit about the limits. Certifying more comparisons does not by itself lower the error of the returned estimate; no guided estimate can be certified closer to the sources than a residual-minimizing data-only fit; and all experiments are synthetic, with validation on real fMRI data left to future work.

      6Heat Constraints from Exponential Correlations and a Sharp Two-Temperature Threshold

      Yuda Bi, Chenyu Zhang, Vince D. Calhoun

      Submitted2026 · companion to paper 7

      Central result

      The total entropy production rate σ of a stationary diffusion observed through one coordinate X is bounded below by the heat QX exchanged with the observed bath and the curvature defect ΔC: the zero-lag curvature of the autocorrelation minus that of an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process with the same variance and diffusion. The bound is sharp for every allowed value of heat and curvature defect.

      Every exponential autocorrelation has ΔC = 0, and positive heat then bounds the entropy production from below. The threshold TH > 2TX additionally assumes conservative forces, no external work, and one common hidden temperature: the hidden environment must be more than twice as hot as the observed bath.

      The question

      A stationary coordinate whose autocorrelation is a single exponential looks like an equilibrium Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process: an equilibrium OU model reproduces the complete Gaussian OU path law even when the hidden dynamics dissipates. A dissipation bound that uses only such a record must therefore allow zero, and bounds built on short-time correlations, correlation times, or cross-correlation asymmetry all vanish for an exact Gaussian OU record. The paper asks what thermodynamics survives inside this blind spot once the heat exchanged with the observed bath is measured independently.

      Results

      • Curvature bound (Eq. 10). For stationary overdamped diffusions with constant diffusion, independent observed and hidden noises, even coordinates, nonlinear forces, and any finite number of hidden coordinates, σ ≥ q2/(2q + ΔC/DX) with q = QX/(kBTX), whenever the denominator is positive. It is the short-time thermodynamic uncertainty relation for the current weighted by the deviation of the observed drift from its variance-matched OU drift, which is optimal among combinations of drift and displacement; the variance sum rule gives its variance. The bound is strict at nonzero heat and a sharp infimum for every allowed heat and curvature defect.
      • Zero curvature defect (Eq. 11). At ΔC = 0, which every exponential autocorrelation satisfies, heat can flow only into the observed bath, and positive heat gives σ > QX/(2kBTX). Neither Gaussianity nor a Markov observation law is needed.
      • Two-temperature threshold (Eqs. 12–14). With conservative forces, constant positive mobilities, no external work, and one common hidden temperature, σ = q(1 − TX/TH), so at ΔC = 0 and QX > 0, TH > 2TX: the coordinate with the exponential record is the colder one, whatever the nonlinear forces and however many hidden coordinates there are. With several hidden baths that all supply heat, the heat-weighted harmonic mean of their temperatures replaces TH; the hottest bath is constrained, the coldest is not.
      • Reciprocal models and finite bandwidth (Eqs. 16–20). A reciprocal two-coordinate model realizes every TH/TX > 2 at fixed observed law and heat, approaching the threshold only as its hidden relaxation rate diverges. In linear conservative networks of any size with one hidden temperature, an exactly exponential record with positive heat couples to exactly two relaxation modes, so a cap Λmax on the relaxation rates raises the threshold to 2 + (2a + q)/(Λmax − a − q).
      • What response and finite data add (Eqs. 22–27). At ΔC = 0 the heat is q = −G′(0+) − a, the initial slope of the response to a small displacement, which the passive record does not contain. Conversely, a five-dimensional OU diffusion whose sampled covariance is exactly exponential at every lag can have a curvature defect of either sign and any size, so certifying ΔC = 0 requires a bandwidth or model assumption.

      Evidence

      A harmonically trapped particle driven by a programmed OU force makes the blind spot concrete. At the tuned stiffness the position spectrum is exactly that of an OU process at the force rate β, so the variance and corner-frequency calibrations agree with each other while both underestimate the stiffness; yet the probe delivers heat QX = kBTX(α − β) > 0 to its bath. With the drive treated as an even coordinate, the entropy production equals this heat in units of kBTX, and the sharp lower limit is half of it.

      The heat must be measured independently, for example through a calibrated response measurement, and the paper quantifies how well conditioned that is. In a simulation that gives the estimator the exact two-coordinate model (q = 2, σ = 5/3), with 250 passive/driven pairs per frequency of 65,537 exactly sampled points each, the single-frequency inverse returns a physical solution in 99.2% of trials at ω = 4 but recovers the heat within 25% in only 66.8% of them; at ω = 0.05 and 64 these fractions fall to 55.6% and 32.0%. The simulation contains intrinsic fluctuations only; sensor noise and calibration error would add to them. Near the tuned stiffness, the statistical evidence against an OU law vanishes quadratically in the detuning while the heat stays finite.

      For an experimental test the paper proposes a temperature scan in coupled thermal RC circuits. With a fixed drift (α = 2/τ, β = 5/τ, g = m = 2/τ), the cold coordinate becomes exactly exponential at TY/TX = 11/4 while heat keeps flowing; with 1 MΩ resistors and τ = 10 ms the capacitors are 5 nF and 10/3 nF. No experiment is reported.

      7Two Probes Recover the Bath Heat That Neither Reveals Alone

      Yuda Bi, Vince D. Calhoun

      Submitted2026 · companion to paper 6

      Central result

      At cancellation, the heat each probe delivers to its bath, in thermal units, is its mechanical response rate αi minus the relaxation rate β of the shared force (the Harada–Sasa relation), with no calibration of the drive amplitude.

      At double cancellation, filtering both records with the same first-order filter, which removes the relaxation of the force, leaves cross-covariance tails (lags k ≥ 1) that decay at the two response rates, one on each side of zero lag.

      The question

      Two harmonically trapped probes, each in its own thermal bath, are driven by one persistent, unobserved OU force. The drive can be tuned so that each position record has exactly the statistics of an equilibrium particle, which defeats every single-channel method; at equal response rates the joint record is also exactly time reversible, which defeats every method based on the arrow of time. The paper asks which response rates and probe–bath heat rates can still be identified from the two passive records.

      Results

      • Two-probe sampled inverse (Eqs. 8–11). At double cancellation (both probes tuned, nonzero drive couplings), each sampled record is a first-order autoregression with coefficient b = e−βΔ. Filtering both records with en = X(nΔ) − b X((n−1)Δ) gives cross-covariance tails proportional to ak−11 at positive lags and ak−12 at negative lags, so consecutive lag ratios determine both response rates, equal responses included. The amplitudes and the zero-lag value are given in closed form.
      • Heat without a time arrow (Eqs. 14–21). The Harada–Sasa relation converts each recovered rate into heat. At equal responses the summed heat in thermal units, 2(α − β), is positive while the observed path irreversibility Π12 is exactly zero: the relative coordinate rejects the common force and relaxes at the response rate. Time-symmetric kinetic bounds are positive in this reversible pair but bound rather than attribute the heat (at (α, β) = (3, 1) the bound is 1.6 and the summed heat rate 4), and near equal response Π12 vanishes quadratically while the heat stays finite.
      • Fisher information (Eqs. 23–26). In the ideal continuous experiment with known diffusion, and with all other parameters estimated jointly, the information one probe carries about its own response rate vanishes quadratically with the weight of the response mode in its covariance, whereas the joint information matrix of the two probes is positive definite at every cancellation point with nonzero forcing, including equal responses. At (α1, α2, β) = (1, 2, 0.5) the joint values per unit time are 0.3582 and 0.01425, against zero for each probe alone. As the forcing vanishes at equal response, with heat rate h, the relative variance of an individual bath heat scales, to leading order, as 2β3/(h4Tobs) and that of the summed heat as 3β/(h2Tobs), for record duration Tobs.
      • Why bath independence is needed (Appendix C 4). With correlated probe noise and a partly shared drive, a fine-tuned family keeps the complete two-channel law while its response rate varies, so the response disappears from the data. An independent response measurement, switching off the drive at unchanged stiffness, or calibrating the drive intensity resolves it.

      Evidence

      Exact-sample likelihood fits on synthetic records, which are given neither cancellation nor the drive amplitude, measure finite-record precision. Across nine designs of 100 stationary pairs (N = 2000, rates (1, 2, 0.5)), neither the cancellation-constrained covariance fit nor the general Kalman likelihood dominates; all 900 fits pass the interval-validity checks, and nominal 90% intervals for the response rates cover 83–94%, the low end not significant after a Holm adjustment.

      At equal responses (1, 1), with unknown and unequal diffusivities and means, 120 records give heat-rate RMSE of 0.12 and 0.15 for true rates of 0.5, and the heat intervals cover 109 and 107 of 120. Across this and a near-equal design (rates 0.95 and 1.05), the median interval widths are 90–94% of the fitted heat: at this duration the heat is identified, not yet precise.

      The paper proposes a two-trap optical test: spheres of radius 0.5 µm at 300 K with α1 = 100 s−1, α2 = 200 s−1, and β = 50 s−1, rms forces of 0.0765 and 0.1711 pN, and bath heat rates of 50 and 150 kBT s−1. For an equal-response configuration (α = 100 s−1, β = 50 s−1, 5 ms sampling), a local asymptotic budget for ideal sampling asks for at least about 19 s of data to reach 20% precision on an individual bath heat, and about 22 min at weak forcing (6 s and 62 s for the summed heat); these durations are necessary, not sufficient. All numerical results use synthetic data; no experiment is reported.

      8A Study’s Own Dual-Regression Maps Cannot Identify Template Mismatch

      Yuda Bi, Zhuolin Li, Jiaxiao Cai, Vince D. Calhoun

      SubmittedIEEE International Symposium on Biomedical Imaging (ISBI), 2027 · companion to paper 9

      Central identity

      In dual regression, W is the stage-1 regression onto the template, and every subject’s own stage-2 maps Ŝi reproduce the template exactly within its span whenever the extracted time courses have full rank, for every subject, template, and sample size. Only the in-span part of a mismatch sets the cross-talk that alters connectivity, and that is exactly the part the own maps replace by the template.

      To first order and without noise, a coupling change on edge (k, l) spills onto an edge (k, b) that shares network k; ρ are latent correlations, and Glb is the mean share of network l’s signal that the template lets into network b’s time course. With ρ dropped, the spill is the fraction Glb of the change. Edges that share no network receive only higher-order terms.

      The question

      Template-based ICA such as NeuroMark extracts every subject’s networks and time courses with one fixed set of group maps, by dual regression (DR) or spatially constrained ICA, so that functional network connectivity (FNC) can be compared across studies. NeuroMark 1.0 was built from young adults, yet it is routinely applied to children and adolescents, whose network topography is still maturing. A study with an imperfect template needs to know how much of a true connectivity change the template keeps, where the rest goes, and whether its own maps can tell. Since networks are usually matched to templates by spatial similarity, the study’s own maps seem the natural place to check the fit. The paper studies DR only; spatially constrained ICA, the NeuroMark default, is not covered.

      Results

      • Proposition 2 (Own maps cannot identify mismatch). If the extracted time courses have full column rank, the stage-2 maps satisfy ŜiW = I exactly. The time courses depend on the true maps only through the cross-talk Mi = SiW, which ignores every part of the maps outside the template’s span, so the part of a mismatch that alters FNC is exactly the part that the own maps replace by the template. Without noise, and with the template’s networks present in the subject, any other cross-talk paired with suitably changed out-of-span maps gives the same own maps. Their spatial correlation with the template, the own-map check, depends only on the residual outside the span.
      • Exact injection (Sec. 2.3). Because stage 1 is linear and the identity holds, adding (A′i − Âi)Ŝi to a subject’s scan changes its time courses under the template that produced Ŝi from Âi to A′i exactly, and under any other template, with stage-1 matrix Wc, by (A′i − Âi)ŜiWc. A coupling change can thus be injected into real scans exactly, and its fate under every template follows from q × q algebra. Under the template that produced the maps, recoloring the time courses to the new correlation matrix leaves every other edge unchanged, to within 10−13 of the source change; under other templates the change spills (Proposition 1).
      • Proposition 1 (Spillover). Writing the fit matrix as Mi = I + G + Di, with G its mean departure from the identity, a change on one edge reaches the edges that share a network with it as in the second display; edges that share no network receive only higher-order terms. At first order the diagonal of G cancels in the retention of the changed edge itself, yet the losses are not small: a heuristic in the form of Spearman’s attenuation formula puts the retention of edge (k, l) near √(okol), where ok is the share of network k’s extracted signal that is its own. An independent template P of the cohort estimates the mismatch as Ĝ = PW − I, which the paper calls the audit.

      Evidence

      The evaluation uses resting-state fMRI from the Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort (8–21 years, 119 volumes, 1,224 participants after quality control). Group ICAs of two disjoint sets of 350 participants, matched to the 53 NeuroMark 1.0 networks, provide the audit template PA and the reference PB through which changes are injected. Eight external templates are then scored on the other 524 participants over 300 injection settings: NeuroMark 1.0 and three perturbed versions of it (smoothed, thresholded, and displaced), NeuroMark 2.1, and the NeuroMark 3.0 development, aging, and infant templates, the last two as wrong-age controls.

      The own-map check followed the residual, not the cross-talk. With every template reduced to its 53 matched components, it ranged only 0.33–0.40, and its ranking of the eight templates correlated with their retention at Spearman −0.24; it was highest for NeuroMark 2.1, which retained only 0.49. Entered with all their components, the four newer templates fell to 0.21–0.24 whatever their retention, and the check then ranked the templates at Spearman 0.81, by the number of components fitted to 119 volumes rather than by what each template loses. These analyses were added after the first held-out results.

      On the 20 common networks, identified by both cohort ICAs and counted for every candidate, NeuroMark 1.0 retained 0.73 of an injected change and PA 0.84, while NeuroMark 2.1 and the development, aging, and infant templates retained 0.49, 0.62, 0.49, and 0.48. The newer templates stayed below NeuroMark 1.0 in every variant tested: entered with all their components, with the reference also entered with all of its components, with the two cohort ICAs swapped, and with network labels from the development maps. NeuroMark 1.0 trailed the cohort ICA only when templates were reduced to matched components (0.87 and 0.87 with all components), and its lead over the development template (0.103, subject-bootstrap 95% interval 0.098 to 0.108) halved with development labels, so part of the adult advantage comes from labeling the networks with adult maps.

      Spillover went where Proposition 1 puts it: for NeuroMark 1.0, edges that share a network with a changed edge held 91% of all spillover targets but made up only 34% of the other edges. Among them, the audit ranked the targets with an average precision of 0.55, against 0.51 for the template’s own spatial overlap and 0.30 for flagging every such edge. The paper concludes that a template’s fit should not be judged from the study’s own DR maps, and that in DR a newer or age-matched template need not keep more of a connectivity change. It is explicit about the limits: retention is measured against a group ICA of the same cohort matched to NeuroMark 1.0, a yardstick that favors templates close to NeuroMark 1.0; the restriction to common networks was adopted after the first held-out results; and only groups that differ in coupling were studied, whereas groups whose maps differ, as across ages, also create spurious differences, the subject of paper 9.

      9Persistence Under Robustness Checks Cannot Tell Topography from Coupling in Connectivity Group Differences

      Zhuolin Li, Jiaxiao Cai, Vince D. Calhoun, Yuda Bi†

      SubmittedIEEE International Symposium on Biomedical Imaging (ISBI), 2027 · companion to paper 8

      † Corresponding author.

      Central result

      A shared check, such as template smoothing or a swap to another template, re-extracts both groups with a new template. Its effect on the group contrast Δ is a mixed second difference: if group-mean FNC depends smoothly on the template and on a group difference of size ε (in maps, coupling, or both), the change is of order εh for a small template change of relative size h. The relative change is O(h) whatever the origin of the difference, so a small shared check can leave both kinds intact.

      Differential alignment changes the template of one group only. It fits a smooth displacement u between the groups’ mean stage-2 maps and re-extracts the moving group with the warped template. Its effect is first order, and because u is fitted to the group difference, it changes the contrast by an amount comparable to the contrast itself.

      The question

      Group differences in functional network connectivity (FNC) from template-based ICA can reflect where networks lie (topography) rather than how they interact (coupling). Groups can differ in topography through atrophy, development, disease, or residual misregistration, and personalizing network locations has reduced the hypoconnectivity found in autism. The usual defense is a robustness check: the finding is shown to persist with another network template, such as another NeuroMark version, and thresholded dual regression (DR), proposed against the bias from overlapping networks, is a further candidate. These checks re-extract both groups alike, although thresholded DR changes each subject’s own maps and so might follow a group’s topography. The paper asks whether persistence under them shows that a difference reflects coupling, and answers with a semi-synthetic benchmark built from real data, in which the origin of every difference is known.

      Results

      • Shared checks (Sec. 2.2). If group-mean FNC depends smoothly on the template and on the group difference, a check that replaces the template for both groups changes the contrast only at second order (first display), so a small shared check can leave either kind of difference intact; the argument bounds only the order, and the constants may differ by origin. Template smoothing by 6 mm is such a small check (h = 0.13), a NeuroMark 2.1 swap (h = 0.87) is not, and thresholded DR, which gives each subject its own effective template, is outside the argument. For any check, persistence raises the odds of a coupling origin by at most 1/P(persist | topographic), a bound reached when every coupling edge stays significant.
      • Differential alignment (Sec. 2.3). If one group’s topography is a deformation of the other’s, re-extracting that group with the template deformed the same way removes the topographic contrast exactly when the deformation acts as a voxel permutation and the noise is exchangeable, and approximately otherwise. The procedure takes as moving group the one whose mean maps deviate more from the template, fits the displacement in the lowest five cosine functions per axis (375 parameters) by damped Gauss–Newton, re-extracts, and refits; three such updates are the default. Updates are needed because stage-2 maps reproduce the template within its span (the identity of paper 8), so a single fit sees only the out-of-span part of the change. A coupling difference that reaches the maps also biases the fitted field.
      • Edge-level reading and screen (Sec. 3.1). Each significant edge’s surviving contrast is read against how much a re-extraction shrinks FNC itself in a group without a difference: a re-extraction is topography-specific when it shrinks topographic contrasts well below FNC while sparing coupling contrasts, and survival near the FNC shrinkage leaves the origin open. This reading was defined after the discovery counts were known. A screen gates differential alignment: the size of the first fitted field must exceed its null from 1,000 site-stratified label permutations (p < 0.05).

      Evidence

      The benchmark draws eight pseudo-group studies from UK Biobank resting-state fMRI (17,584 participants after quality control), each a block of 1,200 participants split into two groups of 600, balanced on site, sex, and age, and into discovery and test halves. Every subject’s data receive an individual smooth random warp, and five arms share subjects and data: zero; a matched sham; S-only, in which group 1 also receives a smooth group displacement (brain RMS 2.8 mm); T-only, in which ten cross-domain edges of group 1 change by Δz = ±0.1, injected through each subject’s NeuroMark 1.0 stage-2 maps using the identity of paper 8; and Both. All 1,378 edges are tested on the test half at a false discovery rate of 0.05.

      Persistence was weak evidence of coupling. In the S-only arm, standard DR made 258 ± 66 discoveries, all topographic, and template smoothing still made 252 ± 68 while keeping the injected coupling edges of the T-only arm. The NeuroMark 2.1 swap made 303 ± 88 and found only 1.0 of the ten coupling edges, partly because the injection is defined in NeuroMark 1.0 space; thresholded DR cut the topographic discoveries by 39% and kept every coupling edge. On the significant edges of the Both arm, persistence raised the odds of a coupling origin only 1.1-, 1.8-, and 1.8-fold under smoothing, the swap, and thresholded DR, with 88% and 56% of topographic edges persisting under smoothing and thresholded DR.

      Thresholded DR separated the two kinds of edge (AUC 0.90) only through a gain: it shrank FNC itself to 0.78 and topographic contrasts about as much (median survival 0.73), while injected coupling gained (1.21), for reasons the paper could not settle. Differential alignment removed topographic contrast specifically: it left FNC nearly unchanged without a displacement (shrinkage 1.00) and kept a median 0.63 of topographic and 1.02 of coupling contrasts (AUC 0.83). In the S-only arm it removed 58 ± 5% of the topographic contrast, against 78 ± 5% for the true field, and left 80 ± 36 discoveries. It has costs. In the T-only arm, updates created off-target discoveries (5.0 ± 7.3 after three); gating on the screen removed them all, since no T-only block passed it, but a matched sham passed in 5 of 8 blocks. In the Both arm the fitted field was poorer, removal fell to 44 ± 8%, and re-extraction made 70 new off-target discoveries per block (52 for thresholded DR), leaving 169 ± 66 against 197 ± 46 for thresholded DR, which found more of the injected edges.

      In FBIRN schizophrenia data (151 patients, 160 controls), the first fitted field was within its permutation null (p = 0.57, descriptive), and the frozen field, applied ungated to the test half, changed the result little (192 significant edges before, 199 after). Under thresholded DR, the 192 edges kept a median 0.85 of their contrast while FNC in the controls shrank to 0.76; relative to that shrinkage (1.12), they fall between the benchmark’s topographic edges (0.94) and its injected coupling (1.55), so their origin remains open. The paper’s rules follow: persistence under a template swap, smoothing, or thresholding is not sufficient evidence of coupling; survival under a re-extraction that keeps the FNC pattern should be read against how much it shrinks FNC in the study’s controls; and the first fitted field should be reported with its permutation null. A field above the null shows that the groups’ maps differ, not that they are displaced; only then is differential alignment applied, with a fixed budget. It is explicit about the limits: the main displacement is one smooth global field, of the class the estimator fits, and a field confined to the default-mode networks was removed about half as well as by the true field; the update budget and other settings were tuned on four of the eight evaluated blocks; and only DR was studied.
